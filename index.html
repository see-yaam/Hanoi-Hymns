    About the math

    The Tower of Hanoi asks: how do you move a tower of stones from one rock to another, one stone at a time, never placing a bigger stone on a smaller one?

    The answer is beautifully recursive. To move n stones from A to C:

    1. move the top n − 1 stones out of the way, A → B (the same puzzle, just smaller)
    2. move the biggest stone, A → C
    3. move the n − 1 stones back on top, B → C

    Each step calls itself on a smaller tower until only one stone is left. The moves double with every stone: 2n − 1 moves in total — 7 for three stones, 255 for eight.

    The fractal rhythm

    Write down which stone moves each time and you get the ruler sequence:

    1 2 1 3 1 2 1 4 1 2 1 3 1 2 1 5 …

    It looks like the marks on a ruler. Each block is two copies of the previous block joined by a new, larger number — the same pattern at every scale, a fractal. In Hanoi Hymns that sequence is the melody and the rhythm: small high stones chime often, big low stones speak rarely, and after a big stone the river takes a longer breath.

    The recursion composes

    The recursion doesn't just play notes — it composes. Every call asks a musical question; every return answers it.

    When a call begins, the harmony steps away from home — a question. The stone it moves is the melody, sung over that chord. When the call returns, the harmony steps back — the answer. Because calls live inside calls, phrases live inside phrases: 1, 3, 7, 15 moves long, each a smaller copy of the whole. The very first call starts at home and the last return brings everything back to the tonic, with a soft bowl strike on each side.

    The constellation: Hanoi is a Sierpinski triangle

    With n stones there are 3n possible arrangements, since each stone can sit on any of the three rocks. Draw every arrangement as a star and connect the ones that are a single move apart, and you get a Sierpinski triangle: the rock holding the largest stone picks one of three corners, the next stone picks a smaller triangle inside it, and so on down to the smallest stone.

    The faint constellation in the sky is that whole map. The amber line is the path your stones are taking, and the shortest solution walks straight along one side of the fractal, from one corner to another.

    How you hear the recursion

    • Stone size → pitch: the largest stone is the lowest note.
    • Destination rock → instrument & position: left, centre, right.
    • Recursion depth → space: deeper calls echo further away.
